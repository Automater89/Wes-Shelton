Running my job search like an AI enablement engagement

Two AI workspaces, written rules about what each one may read and change, one outcome measure, and a person approving every external action. This is the system, what the data showed, and what I changed because of it.

Summary for a busy reader

Who did what. I designed the system, wrote every rule, and approved every change. Claude Code, an AI coding assistant, wrote the scripts and the automation at my direction. I read and review those scripts. I didn't write them by hand. I'm the only user, and none of it is production software.

What it is. A private GitHub repository that holds my verified career facts, a job tracker, a scoring method, resume checks and a set of written procedures (skills) that two AI tools follow. Perplexity does research and reviews. Claude Code scores roles, drafts documents and keeps the tracker. A claude.ai project handles quick questions and sends anything new back as a written proposal. The tools never share a chat. Work passes between them as dated handoff notes with five required parts.

Why it exists. A job search has the shape of an operations problem: postings arrive from several places, every application needs a tailored resume, every claim has to be true, and nothing should go out without a person reading it. I wanted AI to carry the repetitive reading and drafting. I didn't want it inventing a fact or acting for me.

Four controls that do most of the work.

  1. Evidence before generation. A verified baseline of career facts, a register of retracted claims that overrides every draft, and an index of experiences where the ones I confirmed in person are locked so no later AI review can weaken them.
  2. A human at every external action. Nothing is submitted, sent or posted by the system. The tracker changes only from an event I state or a diff I approve, never from an email it read.
  3. One outcome number. Screens or better per ten submitted applications. Every new rule, script or skill has to say which number it moves. If it can't, it goes to a parking lot and I don't build it.
  4. Decision records. A decision log with reasons, a dated status log, a changelog for the tracker, and a twice-monthly review that proposes at most one change the data supports.

The finding that changed the design. After three months I measured the system against outcomes instead of activity. My original fit score did not predict which applications got a screen; the roles I rated highest screened least. The channel (a recruiter or a person I knew, versus a cold portal) mattered more than the score. So I split the score in two (Fit, and separately Screen odds), added a decision called "apply via channel", and made a time-boxed search for a contact the first step before any cold application.

What I would carry to a team. Measure one outcome from day one. Retire a rule every time you add one. Use AI for reading and drafting, and deterministic checks and commit history where you need an audit trail. Keep a person at every action that reaches someone else.

The problem

By mid-2026 I was running a three-lane search (AI enablement, HR and benefits technology, benefits operations) with help from AI tools, and I had the problems any team gets when it adopts AI quickly. Drafts were fast, and sometimes wrong in ways that looked right. A resume once carried a figure I had withdrawn weeks earlier. A tracker lived in one tool, the facts in another, and the two drifted. I couldn't say which of my choices were producing interviews. I was tracking how much I did, and I had no view of what came back.

I treated it the way I'd treat an intake from a team at work: write down the current workflow, name the users (one, me), the constraints (truthful claims, no action without approval, limited hours per week), and the baseline measure. Then design, build small, evaluate, and only then scale or stop.

The engagement cycle, applied to my own workflow

Each role goes through the same six steps. The steps are written down as procedures that the AI tools follow, so the method is reusable and the output is predictable.

  1. Intake Paste the posting. Confirm the apply route is live and tag the posting's age. Pull the requirements into a line-by-line table.
  2. Brief Grade every requirement against the full verified career: strong, undersold, partial, verify, or missing. List what is undersold. Record open questions and knockouts.
  3. Score and decide Compute Fit from evidence coverage, location and pay. Read Screen odds separately. Apply the gates (a suspected knockout forces review). Decide: apply, apply via channel, review, or skip.
  4. Channel first About fifteen minutes looking for a referral, recruiter or warm contact before any cold application. The result is recorded either way. Finding nobody never blocks the application.
  5. Build and check Tailor the resume from the evidence table. Run the coverage check (verified experience the posting asks for but the draft left out), the claim scan (retracted or blocked wording) and the applicant-tracking-system read (reading order, headings, basics in the top third).
  6. Record and learn I submit. The tracker gets one row with the score version, the channel, and the date, in a tag grammar a script can read back. Outcomes are compared with scores on the 1st and 15th.

What the data showed, and what I changed

Three months in, I compared stored scores with real outcomes. Two findings held up.

  • The fit score did not predict results. The roles my rubric rated highest were the least likely to lead to a screen. The score measured how much I wanted the role. It said nothing about how a screener would read my application.
  • The channel mattered more than the score. Applications that went through a recruiter or a personal contact got responses far more often than cold portal applications.

Four changes followed, each logged with its reason.

  • Two scores instead of one. Fit (is this role right for me) and Screen odds (will a screener pull this application), with a new decision, "apply via channel", for roles worth pursuing only with a referral or recruiter first.
  • One number decides. Every new script, skill or rule now has to name how it moves one metric: screens per ten applications. If it can't, it goes to a parking lot.
  • Computed factors with an audit trail. Requirement coverage, location and pay are calculated from the posting. The judgment calls are written down with reasons, so any score can be traced.
  • Cleanup before any new feature. When scoring moved from one tool to the other, an audit found a dozen places in the procedure library that still described the old owner. I fixed the rules, retired two outdated skills, and only then built anything new.

The redesign is still being measured. Weights are frozen through a twenty-application trial, and the question "does the new scoring predict screens better than the old one" gets answered when the sample is large enough to mean something. What I can report today is the method. The result comes when the sample is big enough.

Responsible use, in practice

  • Truthful claims are enforced by the build. A build fails if the resume text contains a blocked figure. A lint step flags proof text that breaks a stated boundary. A pilot stays a pilot in every draft; "deployed" is a blocked word for that project.
  • No manipulation of screening systems. No hidden text, no keyword lists, no inflated years or altered titles. The ATS check tests reading order and whether real qualifications can be found. It is labeled as a formatting read. It doesn't predict whether a resume passes.
  • Minimal data. Form checks use only the fields I paste (experience, education, skills, screening answers), never identity or demographic answers. Collaborators are credited by their role, and their names stay out. Employer internals stay out of public copy, including this page.
  • The person sends. No auto-apply, no auto-send, no status inferred from an email or from silence.
  • Public content is reviewed like a hiring manager would. A scheduled review reads my site, GitHub and LinkedIn as a stranger and lists what to fix or hide. It has already pulled work that promised more than it held.

Keeping it maintainable

  • Delete before you add. Every addition names what it retires. Superseded rules are archived, never left live beside the new one.
  • A value gate, asked once. Before any build: which number does it move, what does it retire, and how much of my time does it cost. Asked at the start, never in the middle of work.
  • Bounded runs. Finish the requested unit in one pass, reuse what exists, ask before expanding scope, stop when the result is verified and usable.
  • A review on the 1st and 15th. Run the calibration, read the logs, propose at most one change the data supports. I approve; it is logged. The first review's finding was "read the dip as timing, not a trend." It proposed a data fix and left the scoring alone.
  • Unmerged work doesn't count. The chat project reads the main branch only, so work is merged or the other tools never see it. A status log at the root records what changed and when.

Limits

What this does not show

  • It is personal work with one user. There are no faculty, staff or customers, and no adoption to measure.
  • I didn't write the code. I specified it, read it, reviewed each change and set the rules it enforces. An AI coding assistant wrote the scripts and the automation.
  • It isn't a product. A separate, scrubbed version for other people is a proposal. It hasn't passed its usability or privacy tests, so it isn't published.
  • The outcome measure is a small sample and still running. I describe the method and the direction of the findings. I don't have a result to claim yet.
  • Figures, employer names, recruiter names and the tracker itself stay private. This page describes the system and keeps its records out.

Worked example, one role end to end

The posting, employer and numbers below are made up to show the shape of the output. Real assessments live in the private repository.

  1. Intake. I paste a posting for a "Benefits Technology Analyst" at a fictional regional health plan. The procedure confirms the apply link is live, notes the posting is nine days old (tag: STANDARD), and pulls fourteen requirement lines into a table.
  2. Brief. Three sample rows from the table:
    RequirementProof from the verified careerGrade
    Test HRIS configuration changes before releaseWritten UAT checklists for a benefits system over several yearsStrong
    Document processes for a benefits teamJob aids written for recurring benefits tasks at handoverUndersold (true, but not on the current resume)
    Administer a specific vendor's platformNot in the recordMissing
    Open question recorded: the posting says "configure" and my record says "test", so that line is marked verify until I can ask.
  3. Score and decide. The script refuses to run until every grade and judgment field is filled. Output (fictional): 
    Fit 7.5 (raw 7.4)   Screen odds: Medium
Gates: 1 basic graded VERIFY (configuration vs testing)
Pay: not posted -> verify
Decision: APPLY VIA CHANNEL
Before you apply: channel check . . . . not recorded  WARNING
  4. Channel first. Fifteen minutes. A former colleague works at the plan's parent company. A short message is drafted in my voice; I decide whether to send it, and I send it myself. The tracker records channel-check=contact-found either way.
  5. Build and check. The resume is tailored from the table. The coverage check prints one line: MISSING: job-aids (triggered by "document processes"), so the job-aids bullet goes back in. The claim scan passes. The ATS read reports five of six basic qualifications in the top third and one date in the wrong format, which gets fixed.
  6. Second reader. A read-only session that never saw the draft rebuilds the evidence table from the files alone and lists any claim it cannot support. It agrees on thirteen lines and flags one undersold item I had not used. I decide whether to add it.
  7. Record. I submit through the contact's referral link and say so. One tracker row is written after the validator passes: 
    [SCORE v2.3 2026-11-06] Fit 7.5 (raw 7.4); odds Medium; APPLY VIA CHANNEL
[SUBMIT 2026-11-06] channel=referral; screening-questions=3; channel-check=contact-found; effort=noted
  8. Learn. On the 1st and 15th the calibration report reads these tags back and groups outcomes by score version, fit band, odds tier and channel. One number is reported, with the pending count beside it, and at most one change is proposed.
