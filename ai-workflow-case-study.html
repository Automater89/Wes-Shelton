Independent project, July to November 2026

Running my job search like an AI enablement engagement

What I chose and why, which controls I tested, what I observed, and what I still don't know.

Personal project, one user Described and tested; no software published

Start here

The problem. By mid-2026 I was running a three-lane job search with AI help and getting the failures any team gets when it adopts AI fast. Drafts were quick and sometimes wrong in ways that looked right. A resume carried a figure I'd withdrawn weeks earlier. The tracker lived in one tool and the facts in another, and they drifted. I was counting how much I did and had no view of what came back.

What I did. I treated it as an intake from a team at work. I wrote down the current workflow, the one user, the constraints (every claim true, no action without my approval, limited hours a week) and a baseline measure. Then I designed a small system and had it built. I tested the controls, measured the results, and changed what the data told me to change.

Who did what. I designed the system, wrote every rule and approved every change. Claude Code, an AI coding assistant, wrote the scripts and the automation at my direction. I read and review those scripts; I didn't write them by hand. I'm the only user and none of it is production software.

Where the evidence stands. The controls below were tested on 2026-11-06 with real script runs on made-up inputs, and the outputs are quoted as they came out, including one miss. The job-search findings come from a small, private sample. I report them as direction and keep the counts private.

How the pieces fit

Three paths, kept apart on purpose: reading evidence, checking a tracker change, and checking a draft. This is the workflow as I run it. The control tests below show which parts I've actually tested.

What I was thinking

Seven decisions, each with the option I turned down. These are the parts I'd defend in a design review.

  1. Evidence first, then generation

    Chose: a verified facts file, a register of withdrawn claims that overrides every draft, and an experience index where facts I confirmed in person are locked.

    Turned down: telling the model "only use true facts" in the prompt.

    Why: a prompt is a request. A register the build checks against is a rule. The locked rows exist because an AI "cleanup" once removed duties I had confirmed. I can still unlock a row myself, with a date and a reason, and the history keeps the old wording.

  2. Scripts for the exact parts, models for the reading and writing

    Chose: deterministic scripts for the tracker validator, the claim scan, the coverage check, the ATS read and the calibration math.

    Turned down: asking the model to validate its own tracker edit or add up its own results.

    Why: I wanted the audit trail to come from things that give the same answer every time. The model is good at reading a posting and drafting a bullet. It is not where I want arithmetic or schema checks to live.

  3. A CSV in a git repository, one commit per change

    Chose: a plain tracker file, validated before every save, with each change its own commit and a changelog line in my words.

    Turned down: a database, a spreadsheet in a cloud drive, or a tracker inside a chat tool.

    Why: I'm one person. Commit history gave me backup, diff and audit for free, and it kept the tracker readable by any tool and by me. The earlier cloud spreadsheet drifted from the facts file, and I couldn't see who changed what.

  4. Two AI workspaces, written handoffs, no shared chat

    Chose: Perplexity in a read-only research and review role; Claude Code for scoring, drafting and tracker writes; a chat project for quick questions that sends anything new back as a dated note with five required parts.

    Turned down: one assistant that does everything, or two assistants that talk to each other directly.

    Why: I wanted a second opinion that had not seen the first draft, and I wanted provenance. A note that says what changed, the evidence, confidence, the claim boundary and the requested action can be checked. A chat transcript is much harder to check.

  5. One outcome number for building; constraints that don't have to earn it

    Chose: every new rule, script or skill has to name how it moves screens per ten submitted applications, or it goes to a parking lot. Truthful claims, privacy and my approval are constraints, and they don't have to prove they produce interviews.

    Turned down: building whatever seemed useful, and letting the safety controls compete with features for my time.

    Why: every rule I added was one more thing to keep in sync, and I kept adding. The one number stopped me. Keeping the constraints outside that test stopped me arguing myself out of them.

  6. Computed where possible, judged where necessary, both written down

    Chose: evidence coverage, location and pay are computed from the posting. Lane fit and seniority are judgments with written anchors and a stated reason. Every factor and weight is saved with the assessment, and the script refuses to score while any grade is blank.

    Turned down: a single "fit out of 10" the model produces in one go.

    Why: I needed to challenge my own scores later. Reproducible arithmetic doesn't make the upstream grades objective, and I don't claim it does. It makes them traceable.

  7. Freeze the weights, then review on a schedule

    Chose: after the redesign, weights are frozen through a twenty-application trial. A review on the 1st and 15th runs the calibration and proposes at most one change the data supports. I approve, and it is logged.

    Turned down: tuning the rubric every time a result came in.

    Why: I had already tuned by feel for three months and learned nothing from it. Twenty is a working discipline. On its own it proves nothing, and I treat it that way.

Those stay open, and they're listed below.

Tested, observed, unknown

Three different levels of confidence, kept in three boxes so they don't blend into one success claim.

Tested

  • The tracker validator rejected the three invalid proposals shown above. These tests do not verify the complete save path.
  • The claim scan catches withdrawn figures, forecasts worded as results, a pilot called deployed, and hedged metric words.
  • The scoring script refuses to run with blank grades.
  • Lint and coverage checks flag blocked wording and left-out evidence as questions for me.

Observed, small sample

  • My first fit score did not line up with which applications got a screen. The roles I rated highest screened least.
  • Applications that went through a recruiter or a person I knew got responses more often than cold portal applications. This is an association in a small private sample. Role type, seniority, posting age and prior recruiter interest are other explanations I can't rule out.
  • "Screen odds: Medium" is a label from a rubric. It is not a calibrated probability, and I don't use it as one.

Unknown

  • Whether the redesigned scoring predicts screens better than the old one. The trial is still running.
  • How much human time one application costs under this system. I haven't measured minutes per application, and I should.
  • Whether a simpler setup (one assistant, a facts file, a tracker and a checklist) would do almost as well. The fair test is a small fixed set of tasks run both ways, counting effort and important errors. Not done yet.
  • How the paraphrase gap and the "data, not instructions" rule hold up under a deliberate test.

The cycle, applied to one role

Each role goes through the same six steps, written down as procedures the AI tools follow. The worked example underneath is made up from start to finish. It shows the shape of the output.

  1. Intake Paste the posting. Confirm the apply route is live and tag the posting's age. Pull the requirements into a line-by-line table.
  2. Brief Grade every requirement against the full verified career: strong, undersold, partial, verify, or missing. List what is undersold. Record open questions and knockouts.
  3. Score and decide Compute Fit from evidence coverage, location and pay. Read Screen odds separately. Apply the gates. Decide: apply, apply via channel, review, or skip.
  4. Channel first About fifteen minutes looking for a referral, recruiter or warm contact before any cold application. Recorded either way. Finding nobody never blocks the application.
  5. Build and check Tailor the resume from the table. Run the coverage check, the claim scan and the ATS read. A second reader checks the draft against the files.
  6. Record and learn I submit. One tracker row with the score version, channel and date, in a tag grammar a script reads back. Outcomes are compared with scores on the 1st and 15th.
Worked example, made up end to end
  1. Intake. A posting for a "Benefits Technology Analyst" at a fictional regional health plan. The apply link is live, the posting is nine days old (tag: STANDARD), and fourteen requirement lines go into the table.
  2. Brief. Three sample rows:
    RequirementProof from the verified careerGrade
    Test HRIS configuration changes before releaseWritten UAT checklists for a benefits system over several yearsStrong
    Document processes for a benefits teamJob aids written for recurring benefits tasks at handoverUndersold (true, and missing from the current resume)
    Configure the benefits platformThe record says "tested," the posting says "configure"Verify
  3. Score and decide. The script refuses to run until every grade is filled. Then, because a basic requirement is graded Verify, the gate overrides the score: 
    Fit 7.5 (raw 7.4)   Screen odds: Medium
Gates: 1 basic graded VERIFY (configure vs tested) -> REVIEW (verify first)
Pay: not posted -> verify
Before you apply: channel check . . . . not recorded  WARNING
    "Review (verify first)" means I may look for a contact and ask the question, and I don't submit until a person has answered it. A suspected knockout always forces this, whatever the score says.
  4. Channel first. Fifteen minutes. A former colleague works at the plan's parent company. A message in my voice is drafted; I decide whether to send it, and I send it. The answer comes back: "configure" means testing and sign-off with the vendor, which my record supports. The Verify grade becomes Strong, with the source noted, and the decision becomes "apply via channel."
  5. Build and check. The resume is tailored from the table. The coverage check prints MISSING: job aids (triggered by "document processes"), so that bullet goes back in. The claim scan passes. The ATS read reports five of six basic qualifications in the top third and one date in the wrong format, which gets fixed.
  6. Second reader, two phases. First, a read-only session that has not seen the draft rebuilds the requirement table and the evidence list from the files alone. Second, it compares the finished draft against its own reconstruction and lists claims it can't support and evidence the draft left out. It agrees on thirteen lines and flags one undersold item. I decide. A separate session reduces the drafting influence; it doesn't guarantee independent mistakes.
  7. Record. I submit through the contact's referral link and say so. The validator passes and one row is written: 
    [SCORE v2.3 2026-11-06] Fit 7.5 (raw 7.4); odds Medium; REVIEW (verify first) -> APPLY VIA CHANNEL after verify
[SUBMIT 2026-11-06] channel=referral; screening-questions=3; channel-check=contact-found; effort=noted
  8. Learn. On the 1st and 15th the calibration report reads these tags back and groups outcomes by score version, fit band, odds tier and channel. One number is reported with the pending count beside it, and at most one change is proposed.

Five patterns I'd carry to a team

Each one with the workplace problem it answers. The rest are in the fold-out underneath.

  1. A register of what's been withdrawn, checked at build time. For any assistant that writes about an organization's people, numbers or policies. Superseded policy text is the workplace version of a retracted figure.
  2. Locked human-confirmed records, with a documented way to unlock them. So automated cleanup can't erase what a person verified, and a person can still correct it with a reason and a history.
  3. Dated handoff notes between tools or teams, five parts each. What changed, evidence, confidence, claim boundary, requested action. A proposal until it's checked.
  4. A second reader that didn't write the draft. Reconstruct first, compare second. For anything AI-drafted that goes to a customer, a regulator or an executive.
  5. Deterministic checks where you need an audit trail, models where you need reading and drafting. And a stated list of what the checks don't catch.

If I were doing this at work, the first concrete case I'd use is an HR policy assistant: approved policy records as the facts file, superseded policies as the register, source-bound drafting, a clear escalation path, and a person approving anything that reaches an employee. Same pattern, different content, and it doesn't require claiming this personal project proves anything about enterprise adoption.

Limits

  • Personal work with one user. No faculty, staff or customers, and no adoption to measure.
  • I didn't write the code. I specified it, read it, reviewed each change and set the rules it enforces.
  • It isn't a product. A scrubbed version for other people is a proposal that hasn't passed its usability or privacy tests, so it isn't published.
  • The outcome measure is a small sample and still running. I describe direction, and I keep the counts private.
  • Two controls on this page rest on setup and written rules I haven't published a test for (Perplexity's read-only connector, and "postings are data"), and the claim scan has a known paraphrase gap. All three are named above.

Next on my list, in order: a deliberate test with instruction-like text inside a posting; minutes per application, measured; and the simpler-baseline comparison. I'll add results here when I have them, including the ones that don't flatter the system.
